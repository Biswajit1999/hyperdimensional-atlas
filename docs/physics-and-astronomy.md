# Physics, astronomy, and what the atlas does not claim

The atlas is a tool for **mathematical** geometry. This document draws the line
between that geometry and physical theory, because the two are routinely conflated.

## 1. Mathematical dimensions versus physical dimensions

A dimension in mathematics is simply an independent coordinate. An n-cube is a
well-defined object in `n` Euclidean spatial dimensions, and we can compute its
structure exactly regardless of whether such a space is physically realised. Whether
the **physical world** has more than three spatial dimensions is a separate,
empirical question. Constructing a tesseract on screen says nothing, by itself, about
the answer.

## 2. Spacetime

Special relativity describes spacetime as three spatial dimensions plus one time
dimension — often written 3+1. It is tempting to call this "four-dimensional" and
equate it with a 4-cube, but the geometry is different. The spacetime interval treats
time with the opposite sign to space:

```
ds² = −c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz²
```

That minus sign makes time **not** interchangeable with a spatial axis. A 4D
Euclidean tesseract and 3+1 spacetime are distinct objects. General relativity goes
further, describing spacetime as a curved four-dimensional manifold whose curvature
is what we experience as gravity.

## 3. Kaluza–Klein and compactification

An early unification attempt added a fifth spatial dimension, curled (compactified)
into a tiny circle, and showed that gravity in five dimensions can reproduce both
gravity and electromagnetism in four. The idea is mathematically elegant and
historically important. It is **not** an established fact: no experiment has revealed
such a dimension.

## 4. String theory and M-theory

Some modern frameworks require ten or eleven total dimensions for internal
mathematical consistency, with the additional spatial dimensions compactified at
extremely small scales. These are **theoretical frameworks**, actively researched but
not confirmed by observation. The atlas does not take a position on them; it simply
notes that "extra dimensions" appears in serious physics as a mathematical device,
not as a measured property of the universe.

## 5. High-dimensional astronomy data spaces

Where high dimensionality is unambiguously real and everyday is in **data**. Working
astronomers routinely operate in many dimensions:

- **Stellar spectra** — each wavelength bin is a coordinate, so a single spectrum is a
  point in a space of thousands of dimensions.
- **Exoplanet population parameters** — mass, radius, period, eccentricity,
  insolation, host metallicity, and more define a high-dimensional parameter space.
- **Gaia astrometry** — positions, parallaxes, and proper motions place a billion
  stars in a rich multi-dimensional measurement space.
- **Cosmological likelihood surfaces** — model fits explore likelihoods over many
  cosmological parameters at once.
- **Machine-learning feature spaces** — learned representations routinely have tens to
  hundreds of dimensions.

We never see these spaces directly. We understand them exactly the way this atlas
treats a hypercube: through projections, slices, correlations, and reduced
representations. The mental habit is identical, which is the real bridge between the
geometry here and working science.

## 6. Evidence versus speculation

The visualiser shows mathematical projections, not photographs or direct views of
extra dimensions. Visual beauty is not evidence. A simulation can build intuition and
sharpen questions, but it cannot replace experimental and observational validation.
Keep two questions apart: *what is the structure of this mathematical object* (settled,
and shown here exactly), and *what does the physical universe actually contain*
(decided in laboratories and observatories, not in a renderer).
